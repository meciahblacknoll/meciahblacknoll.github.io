Where the money actually moves

The inverter is already at the floor — SunGold's $1,422 10kW split-phase unit and ECO-WORTHY's own $1,435 equivalent are within $13 of each other. Stacking two cheaper 5000W units instead costs more ($1,536) and adds a sync headache. The battery is the only real lever: swapping two pre-built 5.12kWh packs ($890 each, $174/kWh) for one hand-built 16S pack from raw EVE LF230 cells drops the landed cost to roughly $139/kWh — and because the fixed cost of a box + BMS doesn't scale with cell size, you end up with more capacity than you asked for, almost for free.

Wiring, end to end

Build order

  1. Top-balance every cell individually
    Bench-charge each of the 16 LF230 cells to 3.65V one at a time before they ever touch a busbar. This is the step DIY builds skip and regret — it's what a pre-built pack does for you at the factory.
  2. Stack and compress
    Cells into the box with fish-paper insulation between faces, end plates on, threaded rod tensioned evenly.
  3. Bolt the busbars
    Series connection across all 16 cells, M6 studs torqued to 8–10 N·m. Check continuity cell-to-cell before power-up.
  4. Wire the BMS
    Balance leads to every cell tap. Confirm all 16 cell readings in the JK app and that they agree with a multimeter before closing the box.
  5. Install main protection
    300A Class-T fuse and the manual disconnect switch go inline on the pack's positive terminal, outside the box.
  6. Mount pack and inverter to the hand truck
    Battery low and centered over the axle, inverter above it, strapped — not just resting.
  7. Wire DC side
    Pack → fuse/disconnect → inverter DC input, 2/0 AWG.
  8. Wire AC side
    Inverter → outlet box (14-50R / TT-30R / 5-20R) with the 6 AWG THHN kit.
  9. Add the PV disconnect
    Inline before the MPPT input, whenever solar panels get added — wire it now even if panels come later.
  10. Power up on the bench, not the road
    Multimeter on pack voltage and every cell tap, BMS app confirming balance, before the inverter ever sees a load.
  11. Ramp the load
    Start low, climb to full 10kW with a clamp meter watching real draw against the 200A BMS ceiling.

Before you touch a busbar

A loose busbar bolt is the single most common DIY LiFePO4 fire cause — it arcs under load instead of carrying current cleanly. Torque every M6 stud to spec and recheck after the first full charge/discharge cycle, once the stack has settled. The 300A main fuse is not optional: a pre-built pack hides this protection inside its case, a hand-built one doesn't, and skipping it is the actual corner this build must not cut.

The saving here is entirely in who you buy from, not in accepting a worse part. The rule that makes it safe: buy "pre-owned," "open-box," or "certified refurbished" listings from a store with a real return policy and high feedback count (the PowMr inverter above is a retailer's own return stock, 99.8% positive, not a stranger's teardown) — and walk away from anything listed "for parts," "not working," or "as-is" from a private seller. That second category exists on the same search (there's a "Fault & Not Work" 10kW inverter listed right next to the good one) and it's a real write-off, not a discount. Before buying any used battery pack, confirm its BMS continuous discharge rating against your actual draw: at 10kW/48V the system pulls ≈208A, so two packs splitting that load need at least 104A continuous each — the 200A-rated golf-cart pack clears that with room, the 100A-rated standard version does not.

Does it pay for itself?

73150 (Del City / OKC) runs on OG&E. On-site solar use nets against the meter at the residential retail rate — about 9.4¢/kWh base-plus-riders, or roughly 13.2¢/kWh all-in once fuel adjustment is added. True excess beyond a billing period only drops to OG&E's avoided-cost buy-back of 3.36¢/kWh off-peak — but at the array size below, a year's production doesn't come close to a typical home's annual usage, so almost everything nets at the retail rate. This assumes the inverter is interconnected under OG&E's NM-1 net-metering tariff (residential systems up to 25kW AC — this build is nowhere near that ceiling). Run purely as a portable, off-grid hand-truck unit instead, and real savings drop to whatever the 10.24kWh pack can actually absorb and discharge before the sun sets — worth keeping in mind if interconnecting isn't the plan.

Panels at $0.40/W bulk-pallet (10×400W, $1,600) plus mount: $800 in posts/rail/footings for the fixed rack, $1,200 for a single ganged linear-actuator tracker frame (actuator, steel pole and bearing, limit switches, an ESP32 light-sensor or astronomical controller), $2,000 for a second axis and gearing on top of that. Energy gain over fixed: +20% single-axis, +35% dual-axis — both typical for this latitude.

Single-axis is the one worth building. It ties the fixed rack's payback almost exactly (3.4 vs 3.5 years) while banking $136 more every year after that, for life — the extra yield pays for the tracker's own added cost on roughly the same timeline the fixed rack needed anyway. Dual-axis produces more energy but its payback is slower than fixed (3.9 yrs) and its 10-year edge over single-axis is only $220 — not much margin against a second motor, a second axis of bearings, and twice the weatherproofing sitting outside for a decade. Build one axis, not two.
