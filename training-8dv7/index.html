Bedroom Training Program

A five-day structure you repeat for years. Joint health wins every tie-break; the planche, handstand push-ups and both splits are the skill line; a Reeves-proportioned "Greek statue" build is the aesthetic line. Everything below is built from one complete set of baseline measurements taken 2026-11-21.

01The three goals

Three goals, in strict priority order. When two of them pull in different directions, the higher one wins. You chose this order yourself, and you chose it again when asked directly which one wins a conflict.

PRIORITY 1 · WINS EVERY TIE

Joint health

Joints feel protected and good every session. Tendons, ligaments and cartilage get better over years, never traded away for a faster skill or a bigger lift. Currently no joint pain; keep it that way.

PRIORITY 2

Skill and strength

Full front split. Full side split. Full planche. Three or more strict handstand push-ups against the wall.

PRIORITY 3

Greek-statue build

Built on purpose, not just bigger. No "skinny legs." No specific numeric aesthetic goal — Section 04 uses one well-sourced reference point (Reeves) for context on proportion, not as a build to match.

The override

Any new ache that lingers past 48 hours drops that movement back one tier, or out of the session entirely, for that week. This overrides every other rule in this document, including progression targets, with no exceptions. It is the operational form of Priority 1.

Context the program is built around. Returning lifter: push-ups and pull-ups are fine, prior barbell experience, just out of practice. Up about 20 lb since the relationship started. Trains in a bedroom that becomes a gym when the Murphy bed folds up. Wants 4–5 sessions a week of 45–60 minutes, repeating the same structure indefinitely with progressive overload. No variety-seeking: the structure stays, the numbers move.

03Leverage and build

Torso-to-leg proportion: average. A Cormic index of 51.0 % against a population mean of about 52 % is an unremarkable, middle-of-the-distribution build. Legs at 49.0 % of height are neither long nor short. There is no "short legs, long torso" story here; a previous draft said there was, but that conclusion came from the erroneous earlier sitting-height reading and is retracted. Practical consequence: no squat-stance or deadlift-style adjustments are indicated by proportion. Set up however feels best on your hips and ankles.

Arm length: slightly long, and it barely matters. Wingspan exceeds height by 2.8 cm (1.1 in). Longer levers make the planche and the bench press marginally harder to leverage; on pull-ups and rows the only effect is a slightly longer range of motion to cover, which is roughly neutral rather than an advantage. Treat this as a footnote, not a design input. Nothing in the program is changed because of it.

Wrist and ankle: average frames, so Reeves' ratios apply cleanly. The wrist (18.0 cm) and ankle (22.5 cm) are the two fixed reference points in the proportion framework below. Both sit in the normal male range, which means the Reeves multipliers produce targets that are ambitious but not structurally impossible.

04Proportion framework (resolved)

The aesthetic goal is a classical, Greek-statue build. The modern lifter most associated with that look is Steve Reeves (Mr. America 1947, Mr. Universe 1950), and his published measurements and proportion formula are the natural source for targets. An earlier draft used five "classical ratios" attributed to Reeves. Two of them were not Reeves at all. This section fixes the sourcing and makes a decision.

How this is actually used

Reference point, not a target

Important framing correction, 2026-11-21: this whole section exists because "how do people actually build a Greek-statue look" was an open research question, not because the goal is to match Steve Reeves specifically. Reeves is one well-documented 20th-century bodybuilder's personal interpretation of classical proportion, not the classical statues themselves, and nothing here is "the" Grecian ideal in any authoritative sense. His three skeletal-anchored ratios (arm=calf=neck, arm:wrist 2.52, calf:ankle 1.92) are kept below as one reasonable, well-sourced reference point for understanding proportion — useful context for reading your own numbers, not a build to chase. No part of this program is "done" or "not done" based on how close these numbers get to his.

The other two drafted ratios (shoulders:waist 1.618, chest:waist 1.4-1.5) are dropped entirely rather than fixed, for a separate reason: neither has any defensible classical source at all — one is modern marketing, the other a mis-transcription — so there was never a real number to anchor them to in the first place.

05Governing principles

Why the program is shaped the way it is. Each of these shows up as a concrete rule somewhere below.

  1. Connective tissue adapts slower than muscle. Tendons and ligaments remodel over months, muscle over weeks. A program that lets strength run ahead of tendon capacity produces exactly the kind of lingering ache that Priority 1 forbids. So: smallest increments, progression gated on clean reps, and heavy shoulder/wrist loading never on consecutive days.
  2. Joint stress is counted per joint, per week, not per exercise. Bench, overhead press, dips, pike/handstand push-ups and planche leans all load the same shoulder and the same extended wrist. The week is laid out so those sit on three non-adjacent days (Mon / Wed / Fri), with pull-and-lower days between them. Section 07 shows the joint map.
  3. Every pattern twice a week, really. Horizontal push, horizontal pull, vertical push, vertical pull, squat, hinge each appear on at least two different days (vertical push lands on three, via the handstand push-up ladder). The previous draft claimed this and quietly missed it for the pull patterns; the table in Section 07 is the proof for this version.
  4. Skill is practised fresh. Planche and handstand push-up progressions live on a dedicated mid-week day with low systemic fatigue, stopped well short of failure, because motor learning degrades when tired and tendons are most at risk when form breaks.
  5. Hard stretching is training. Loaded and PNF split work is recovery-limited like lifting. It is capped at three intense sessions a week on non-consecutive days (Mon / Wed / Fri, when the legs are not freshly squatted), with a gentle daily touch everywhere else.
  6. The barbell is the long-term tool. It has no practical weight ceiling and is being bought used and cheaply. The 35 lb adjustable dumbbells are already too light for a returning lifter's main lifts; they stay for lateral raises, calf work and wrist prehab, and no larger dumbbell set should be bought.
  7. Volume lands in the evidence range and stays there. 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week, 1–3 reps shy of failure. Calves are deliberately dosed at 14 sets because the calf is a flagged size gap and gets almost nothing from the skill work.
  8. Sleep is a training input. 7–9 hours. Tendon and ligament remodeling happens disproportionately during sleep; a short night is a missed session for the joints even if the lifting happened.
  9. Repeat, don't rotate. The structure does not change. Only the loads, reps, hold times and tiers move. Deloads are scheduled, not improvised.

07The week

Five sessions, Monday to Friday, weekend off. The order is chosen so that the three days that load the shoulder in pressing and the wrist in extension (Mon, Wed, Fri) are never back to back, and the two pull-and-lower days (Tue, Thu) sit between them. Every session starts with the daily layer.

Daily layer, every session and every rest day

5–10 min of joint CARs (controlled articular rotations: neck, shoulders, elbows, wrists, spine, hips, knees, ankles) before anything else, every session. A gentle, not maximal, split-stretch touch every day, 3–5 min each for front and side split positions, including rest days. The hard split work lives inside Mon / Wed / Fri below, never as a second daily max-effort session.

Drop Friday (Push B) that week. Never drop Wednesday (it is the whole skill goal) and never drop a pull day (it is what keeps the shoulders balanced against the pressing). 08Progression rules

Loaded lifts: double progression

  • Compounds (bench, press, squat, deadlift, RDL, rows, weighted pull-ups): 6–12 reps. Isolation and calves: 10–20 reps.
  • Work at 1–3 reps shy of failure. The last rep should look like the first.
  • Start a new weight at the bottom of the range. Add reps session to session. When every set hits the top of the range, add the smallest increment available (2.5 lb per side on the bar; one backpack plate for pull-ups) and start again at the bottom.
  • Reps-first vs load-first progression were compared in a 2022 controlled trial and performed about equally, so this simpler reps-first default is fine; there is nothing to gain from a more elaborate scheme.
  • 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week, which the Section 07 table already satisfies for the upper body; the lower body sits at the floor of the range by design and is raised one set at a time when it stalls.

Bodyweight skills: the tier rule

Advance a tier only after 3–4 sets of 8–12 clean reps, or a clean 30-second hold, at the current tier (Steven Low, Overcoming Gravity). "Clean" means the last set looks like the first: locked elbows, no hip sag, no arching, no wrist pain. Falling short on a new tier for two straight sessions means go back one tier for a week, then retry.

Deload

Every 6–8 weeks, or immediately on a two-session stall on two or more lifts, take one week at about half volume: same exercises, same loads, half the sets, skill ladders held at the current tier with fewer sets, splits at the gentle daily level only. Put the deload on the calendar in advance; do not wait to feel like you need it.

Sleep

7–9 hours, treated as a programmed input. Tendon and ligament remodeling happens disproportionately during sleep, so a run of short nights is a connective-tissue deficit even if every session was completed. If sleep is short for a week, treat it like a deload week for the skill ladders: hold the tier, do not test a new one.

Joint-health override, restated

Any new ache lingering past 48 hours drops that movement back one tier or out of the session entirely for that week. Overrides double progression, overrides the tier rule, overrides the deload calendar. No exceptions.

09Skill ladders

Each ladder is climbed with the tier rule from Section 08. Timelines are honest ranges for a returning lifter with your starting point, not promises.

Planche

Realistic timeline to a full planche: 1–3 years. Trained Wednesday (main) and Monday (primer).

  1. Planche lean: hands on the floor, shoulders travel forward past the wrists, hollow body, feet on the ground. 3–4 sets of 30 s clean, shoulders well past the wrists
  2. Tuck planche: knees tucked to chest, feet off the floor, hips at shoulder height. 30 s clean hold
  3. Advanced tuck planche: back flat, hips opened to roughly 90°. 30 s clean hold
  4. Straddle planche: legs straight and wide. 15–20 s clean hold
  5. Full planche: legs together. goal reached; then build to 10 s

Handstand push-up

Realistic timeline to 3 strict wall handstand push-ups: 4–8 months. Trained Wednesday and Friday.

  1. Pike push-up: feet on the floor, hips high, head to the floor between the hands. 3–4 sets of 10 clean reps
  2. Elevated pike push-up: feet on the bench, torso near vertical. 3–4 sets of 10 clean reps
  3. Wall HSPU negative: kick up to a wall handstand, lower under control to the head touching, kick down. 5 sets of 3 reps with a 3-second controlled lower each
  4. Partial-range wall press: press out of the bottom quarter, then half, then three-quarters, using stacked books or a block under the head to set the depth. 3 sets of 5 at 25 %, then 50 %, then 75 % of the range
  5. Full wall handstand push-up. 3 or more strict reps: goal reached

Splits

Realistic timelines: front split 3–6 months if you are already comfortable near the floor, up to about 12 months typically; side split 6–18 months, genuinely longer. Intense sessions Monday, Wednesday (main) and Friday; gentle daily touch everywhere else.

  • Method: 30–60 s static holds, PNF contract-relax (contract into the floor 5–10 s, release, sink), light loaded stretching once holds are stable. Dedicated session 15–25 min (Wednesday); shorter 10–12 min sessions Monday and Friday.
  • Cap: intense work 3–4 times a week maximum, non-consecutive days, because it is recovery-limited like lifting. Be aware this specific frequency cap is a practical coaching heuristic rather than something with the same evidence base as the volume and progression rules above; it is the conservative choice, which is what Priority 1 asks for.
  • Pass criteria: front split is "full" when the back knee and front hamstring both rest on the floor with the hips square; side split is "full" when the inner thighs rest on the floor with the hips stacked over the knees. Partial milestones to log: hand-support height needed (blocks, then fingers, then none).
  • Joint rule applies: stretching sensation belongs in the muscle belly, never in the knee, the inside of the hip joint, or the sitting bone tendon attachment. Pain in any of those is a 48-hour-rule event.

10Measure and re-measure

Monthly, same morning conditions, same tape, same landmarks as the baseline table: shoulder circumference, chest, waist, neck, upper arm flexed, forearm, thigh, calf. Recompute Navy body fat from neck, waist and height. Do not re-measure height, wingspan, sitting height, wrist or ankle; they are fixed and the ratios depend on them staying fixed.

What to look for each month:

  • Arm, calf and neck drifting closer together. Simplest, most direct read on balanced development, worth watching regardless of any outside reference.
  • Arm ÷ 18.0 and calf ÷ 22.5 moving up over time. The Reeves figures (2.52, 1.92) are there for context on scale, not as a finish line.
  • Waist moving down while shoulder circumference and chest move up. Direction is what matters here, never a target number.
  • Thigh holding or rising. If it falls while the waist falls, quad and hamstring volume goes up before anything else changes (no skinny legs).

Weekly, in the training log: every working set's load and reps, every skill tier's best hold or rep count, splits hand-support height, hours slept, and any ache with its start date (the 48-hour rule needs the date).

11Still open

  • Squat rack or squat stands. A genuine equipment gap. Without them there is no conventional back squat and no bench press with a proper bar path; the Zercher / front squat / floor press substitutions in Section 06 are good but are substitutions. Used squat stands plus the existing flat bench cover both lifts and are the cheapest fix; keep the Marketplace watch running alongside the barbell purchase.
  • Barbell and plates: not yet acquired. The program is written in barbell notation because that is the near-term plan; the "no barbell yet" column in Section 06 is in force until it lands.
  • The arm = calf = neck convergence point. Stated as 37–38 cm (14.6–15.0 in) at today's neck; it will drift upward as the neck thickens. It is re-set at each monthly re-measure rather than fixed here.

Everything else that earlier drafts left open (ratio sourcing, the shoulder/wrist clustering, pull-pattern frequency, calf volume, the sitting-height and ankle questions, the leg:torso claim) is resolved in this document.
