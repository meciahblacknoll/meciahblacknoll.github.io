Bedroom gym · Phase 1 · built, not bought

A training skeleton, not a workout

This is the governing logic and a first real weekly structure — built around your stated goals, returning-lifter baseline, and the rule that joint health wins every tie-break. It's deliberately not yet anatomy-tailored in the fullest sense; the open items at the bottom are what's still missing for that.

The three goals, as stated

Aesthetic
  • Greek-statue proportions — balanced, built, not just big
  • No skinny legs — legs get real direct volume, not an afterthought
  • No fixed numeric target; this gets more specific once there's a visible baseline
Skill / strength
  • Full front split, full side split
  • Full planche
  • 3+ strict handstand push-ups
Health
  • Joints feel protected, safe, good — not sacrificed for the other two
  • Connective tissue gets stronger over years, never just "good enough for now"
  • This goal wins any conflict with the other two, by your own call

Why this changes where effort goes, not just what gets measured: the shoulder:waist ratio responds mainly to width work — overhead pressing, lateral raises, vertical pulling (Day 4, Upper B) — more than to bench press, which mostly adds chest thickness rather than shoulder width. The waist side of that same ratio is almost entirely a body-composition question, not a training one — ab work (already present via hollow holds/L-sits) adds visible definition but doesn't shrink the actual circumference. And the arm=calf=neck rule is a hard numeric version of the "no skinny legs" goal: it's not satisfied by legs that merely look okay, it's satisfied by a calf measurement in the same range as the arm measurement. Real finding, 2026-11-19: the two arm targets already disagree — the neck-match rule asks for 37cm, the wrist-ratio rule asks for 45cm. That's not a contradiction to resolve by picking one; it means your wrist is proportionally larger relative to your neck than the classical statues' average build, so a single body part (the neck, which barely responds to training) can't be the only anchor — the wrist-ratio target is the more informative one here since it's the more training-independent of your two "fixed" bones. Worth remembering once shoulder width and chest come in: this is a build with its own specific proportions, not a perfect match to a textbook ratio, and that's fine — the point of measuring both is catching exactly this kind of thing instead of chasing one number blind.

Leverage reading, 2026-11-19: height 182.2cm (71.73in), wingspan 185cm (72.83in) — wingspan exceeds height by 2.8cm (1.1in), a mild positive "ape index." Why it's structured this way

01
Two different overload mechanisms, running at once. Barbell/dumbbell lifts progress the normal way: hit the top of your rep range, add weight next time. Bodyweight skill lines (planche, handstand push-ups, splits) don't have that dial — they progress through leverage tiers instead. The rule that governs when you're allowed to move to a harder tier: 3-4 sets of 8-12 clean reps, or a clean 30-second hold, at the current tier, before advancing. This is the standard from Steven Low's Overcoming Gravity, the reference text for exactly this kind of progression.
02
Joint health is a pacing rule, not a separate workout. Tendons and ligaments adapt 2-3× slower than muscle. Practically: every session opens with a few minutes of joint-specific CARs (controlled articular rotations) for whatever you're about to load, and the progression rule above is never skipped for being "ready early." A new ache that lingers past 48 hours knocks that movement back a tier that week — no exceptions. That's what "joint health wins the tie-break" actually means in practice, not just as a stated value.
03
Movement patterns, not muscle groups, organize the week. Every pattern — horizontal push/pull, vertical push/pull, squat, hinge, core — gets hit roughly twice a week, which is the frequency sweet spot in the hypertrophy literature and also what keeps a repeating week from quietly neglecting legs (direct cause of "skinny legs").
04
Flexibility doesn't fit the same weekly rhythm as strength. Real split progress comes from near-daily stretch exposure (a time-under-stretch adaptation), not a once-a-week session. So split work shows up in small doses every day as a baseline layer underneath the whole week, with one dedicated longer block on its own leg day.
05
Skills get practiced 2-3×/week, not once. Planche and handstand push-up work appear on two different days each (once as the main focus, once as a lighter second touch), because skill acquisition responds better to frequent, sub-maximal practice than to one hard weekly session.

Non-negotiable, every single day

5-10 min of joint CARs (shoulders, hips, spine, wrists, ankles) before anything else, and a gentle 5-min split touch (well short of max tension) even on full rest days. The dedicated hard split work lives inside the weekly template instead (3 sessions/week combined across Lower A, Skill, Lower B) — intense PNF/loaded stretching is recovery-limited just like lifting, so "daily maximal stretching" would actually work against progress, not for it.

Progression rules — the actual dial

Barbell / dumbbell lifts — double progression

Compound lifts (bench, squat, deadlift, press, rows) work a 6-12 rep range; isolation/accessory work (lateral raises, curls) works 10-20. Hit the top of the range for every set → add the smallest available increment next session, then drop back to the bottom of the range and repeat. This is the method the actual research supports: a 2022 controlled trial found reps-first and load-first progression perform about equally for both strength and hypertrophy over 8-10 weeks, so there's no real penalty for defaulting to this simpler version instead of something more complicated. Target 10-20 hard sets per muscle group per week across the program, each set finishing 1-3 reps short of failure — that volume range is the current evidence-based sweet spot, not a round number.

Bodyweight skill lines (planche, HSPU, handstand, splits)

Advance to the next progression tier only after 3-4 sets of 8-12 clean reps, or a clean 30-second hold, at the current tier (exact pass criteria per tier are in the ladders below). Never sooner, even if it starts feeling easy early — the connective tissue adapting underneath the movement is slower than the muscle feeling ready.

Joint-health override

Any new ache lingering past 48 hours drops that specific movement back a tier, or out of the session entirely that week. This overrides both of the rules above, every time, no exceptions — this is what makes "joint health wins" a mechanism rather than a slogan.

Skill progression ladders

The actual named steps for each skill target, not just "work up to it" — each step has a real pass criterion that triggers moving to the next one.

Planche

StepPass criterion to advance
1. Planche leanBuilds wrist/shoulder tolerance first — no hold target, just comfortable loaded wrists at increasing lean angle
2. Tuck planche30s clean hold, knees fully tucked to chest
3. Advanced tuck planche30s clean hold, knees away from chest but still bent

Realistic timeline: first clean tuck hold around 3-6 months in; full planche typically 1-3 years depending on starting strength and bodyweight. This is a genuinely long skill line — the 2-3×/week practice frequency in the weekly template matters more than any single session's intensity.

Front & side splits

This one works differently from the other two — it's not a strength-leverage ladder, it's a stretch-tolerance adaptation, and the evidence changes how often it should actually be trained hard vs. just touched.

ParameterEvidence-based target
Static hold duration30-60s per stretch, 15s minimum
Session length (dedicated work)15-25 min
Intense PNF / loaded stretchingCapped at 3-4×/week — recovery-limited like any other hard tissue stimulus, more isn't automatically better
Light daily maintenanceA few gentle minutes is fine and useful every day; this is NOT the same session as the capped hard work above
Front split timeline3-6 months if already comfortable sitting on the floor; up to 12 for most adults
Side split timeline6-18 months — genuinely the longer of the two

This corrects something in the original weekly template above: "daily hard stretching" was the wrong framing. The template's dedicated split blocks (Lower A, Skill day, Lower B) already land at 3×/week combined, which matches the evidence-based cap well — the daily layer should be read as a light touch only, not a second hard session. Side split currently gets 2 dedicated touches/week to front split's 1; worth adding a front split set to the Skill day alongside the existing side split work so both land at the same frequency.

Still open before this is truly anatomy-tailored

  • Your actual current handstand hold time, split depth (fist-widths from floor), planche-lean tolerance, and HSPU regression tier — these set the real starting rung on each skill line, rather than a generic starting point
  • Any shoulder/hip structural tendencies that make one skill line more natural than another for your build specifically — needs either real testing or just noticing what feels disproportionately easy or hard once this is running
  • Equipment timeline — this assumes a used barbell + plates + some kind of rack show up; goblet squat and dumbbell press substitutes are already written in above as the fallback until then
