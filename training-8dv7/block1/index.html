Training Block One

Weeks 1–12 of the bedroom program. The five-day week, the exercises, the progression rules, the skill ladders and the measurements are exactly as the base document settled them; this page adds the calendar on top: which weeks load, which week backs off, which week measures, and what should realistically have moved by the end. 01What this adds, and what it leaves alone

The base document (Bedroom Training Program v2) is the authority on what you do: the three goals in priority order, the 13 baseline measurements, the Monday Push A / Tuesday Lower + Pull A / Wednesday Skill / Thursday Lower + Pull B / Friday Push B week, double progression, the tier rule, the deload rule, the sleep rule, the 48-hour joint-health override, the three skill ladders, and the Reeves ratios as a de-emphasised reference point rather than a target. Nothing on this page re-opens any of that. Where this page and the base disagree, the base is right and this page has a bug.

What the base deliberately does not say is when. "Every 6–8 weeks" for the deload, "monthly" for the tape, "1–3 years" for the planche. Those are correct as rules, but a rule is not a calendar. This block puts the rules on one: twelve named weeks, one scheduled deload, one scheduled retest, and an honest statement of how far each ladder should have moved by the end.

Why a 12-week block when the week never changes

  • The deload rule only works if it is on the calendar. The base says "put the deload on the calendar in advance; do not wait to feel like you need it." This is that calendar. Week 7 is the deload whether or not week 6 felt hard.
  • Twelve weeks is three monthly tape measurements. Weeks 4, 8 and 12 are the base document's monthly re-measure days, so a block lines up exactly with three data points, and the week-12 retest is the third one with a few extra tests bolted on.
  • Skill timelines need checkpoints, not just end dates. "4–8 months to three handstand push-ups" is honest but unactionable week to week. Section 04 translates each ladder into "where you should plausibly be at week 6 and week 12," so a stall is visible early and a fast run is not mistaken for permission to skip tiers.
  • The research you asked about has programming consequences. The CARs-to-FRC connection, PAILs/RAILs, the Nippard warm-up, and the flexibility-mortality literature each change a specific line of the daily layer or the splits work. Those changes are additive and are introduced on a schedule inside this block (Sections 06 and 07) rather than all at once.
Still the override

Any new ache that lingers past 48 hours drops that movement back one tier, or out of the session entirely, for that week. Nothing in this block, including the week-by-week expectations, the PAILs/RAILs introduction, or the week-12 retest, outranks this. If the retest week lands on a sore wrist, the wrist tests are skipped and logged as skipped.

02Shape of the block

Six weeks of loading, one deload, four more weeks of loading, one retest week. The deload at week 7 is the base document's "every 6–8 weeks" rule at its earliest point, chosen because this is a returning lifter's first block and connective tissue is the thing being protected. The retest week is itself run at reduced volume, so the span from week 7 to the next full deload (Block 2, week 7) contains a light week in its middle.

Why the deload is half-volume and not a week off

The only randomised trial that has tested a one-week deload directly (Coleman et al., 2024, PeerJ) used a complete week off in week 5 of a 9-week programme in resistance-trained young adults (about 21–22 years old, three-plus years of training). Muscle growth was the same whether the week off was taken or not; lower-body strength gained a little more in the group that trained straight through. The authors themselves noted that coaches almost never deload by stopping completely, and that reduced-volume deloads were not tested. Two conclusions follow for this block. First, a deload will not cost you muscle, so there is no reason to skip it. Second, the half-volume version the base document prescribes keeps you lifting, which is the version least likely to show the small strength dip, and the one most compatible with the point of the deload here: connective tissue, which those trials did not measure at all.

03Week by week

Every week is the same five sessions from the base document. What is written below is only what changes that week: load expectations, which rules are in play, what to log, and what enters the mobility layer. Where a week says nothing about a lift, run double progression as normal.

Week 1

Nov 23 – 27
placement
  • Loads. Every compound starts at a weight you can do for the bottom of its range (6 for the 6–12 lifts, 10 for the 10–20 lifts) with 3 reps clearly in reserve. If in doubt go lighter; you will add reps next week anyway. Barbell lifts use the "no barbell yet" column of the base substitution ladder until the bar lands.
  • Ladder placement. Wednesday: test each ladder's tier 1 pass criterion once, fresh. Planche lean 30 s clean? Pike push-up 10 clean? If yes, you are at tier 2 next week; if no, tier 1. Do not test tier 2 the same day.
  • Splits. Record hand-support height for front split (both sides) and side split: blocks, fingers, or none. This is the single number that tracks splits all block.
  • Longevity markers, once. Section 08 explains why; they take five minutes. Sitting-rising test score (0–10), 10-second one-leg stance each side, standing toe-reach distance to the floor, knee-to-wall ankle dorsiflexion distance each side. Log and forget until week 12.
  • Warm-up. From today every session opens with the 3-minute pulse-raiser then CARs (Section 06).

Week 2

Nov 30 – Dec 4
placement
  • Loads. Add reps, not weight. A returning lifter usually finds week-1 weights were too light; if a set hits the top of the range with reps to spare, that lift may add the smallest increment now, otherwise hold.
  • Technique bank. Film one set of each main lift from the side this week. It is the comparison footage for week 12, and the "last rep looks like the first" check is easier against video than memory.
  • Sleep log begins to matter. From here, the base rule applies: a week averaging under 7 hours means no new tier is tested that week.

Week 3

Dec 7 – 11
loading A
  • Loads. First real load increases on the most practised patterns (pull-ups via backpack, rows, RDL). Smallest increment, back to the bottom of the range.
  • Skill. First realistic week for a tier-1 to tier-2 move on the HSPU ladder if pike push-ups were already comfortable. Planche stays on the lean. Nothing moves faster than the tier rule allows.

Week 4

Dec 14 – 18
loading Atape · month 1
  • Monthly tape (Friday morning). The eight training-responsive measurements, same tape, same landmarks as the baseline table. Recompute Navy body fat. Expect noise, not signal, at four weeks: the honest read is "waist flat or slightly down, nothing else distinguishable from measurement error."
  • Splits. PAILs/RAILs enter on Wednesday only, replacing the "light loaded stretching" line of the Wednesday session, at no more than 50 % effort (Section 07 has the procedure). Monday and Friday stay as written.
  • Loads. Bench/floor press, squat variant and deadlift typically take their first increment around here.

Week 5

Dec 21 – 25
loading A
  • Holiday week. If it collapses to four slots, drop Friday Push B per the base rule, never Wednesday, never a pull day. Do not move Friday's work into another day.
  • Loads. Normal double progression. If two lifts have stalled for two sessions each, the base rule says deload immediately; here that means pulling week 7 forward to week 6, not adding a second deload.

Week 6

Dec 28 – Jan 1
loading A · last before deload
  • Loads. Last loading week before the deload. Do not chase a number because the deload is coming; the deload is not a reward for a heavy week.
  • Skill checkpoint. Plausible state: HSPU ladder at tier 2 (elevated pike) or finishing tier 1; planche at a clean 30-second lean, possibly first tuck attempts; splits one support level lower than week 1 on at least one side. If you are ahead of this, good; the tier rule still governs. If behind, nothing changes yet.
  • Write down week-6 loads. Week 8 restarts at these exact loads and reps.

Week 7

Jan 4 – 8
deload
  • Everything at half the sets, same loads, same exercises. 4 × 6–12 becomes 2 × 6–12 at the week-6 weight. Reps stay in the range; this is not a week to grind.
  • Ladders held. Current tier, half the sets, no testing. Monday's planche-lean primer stays at 2 sets.
  • Splits. Gentle daily touch only. No intense sessions, no PNF, no PAILs/RAILs. The CARs daily layer continues in full: it is the lowest-load thing in the programme and the one that should never be skipped.
  • Why now. Six weeks of loading is the earliest the base rule allows, and it is deliberately early for a first block. The deload is for tendons and ligaments, which do not announce fatigue the way muscles do.

Week 8

Jan 11 – 15
loading Btape · month 2
  • Loads. Resume at the week-6 loads and reps. Most lifts feel noticeably easier than they did in week 6; that is the deload working, and the right response is to add reps, not to jump weight.
  • Monthly tape (Friday morning). Second data point. By eight weeks the waist trend is usually readable; arm, calf and neck convergence (the base document's simplest read) may or may not be.
  • Splits. PAILs/RAILs now on Wednesday and Friday, effort up to 70 %. Monday stays PNF contract-relax. Three intense sessions a week, non-consecutive, unchanged from the base.

Week 9

Jan 18 – 22
loading B
  • Loads. Double progression. This and week 10 are where the post-deload bump usually shows as a load increase on the main lifts.
  • Skill. Second HSPU tier move becomes plausible for the first time (elevated pike to wall negatives). Only if the pass criterion is met clean across 3–4 sets.

Week 10

Jan 25 – 29
loading B
  • Loads. Double progression. If a lift has stalled since week 8, the base document's first fix applies before anything else: for the squat, a fifth set; for other lifts, hold and keep adding reps.
  • Equipment check. If the barbell and plates have landed, move each lift one column left on the substitution ladder and re-find the bottom-of-range load for the new variation. A new exercise is a new double-progression run, not a continuation.

Week 11

Feb 1 – 5
loading B · last full week
  • Loads. Last full loading week of the block. Nothing special; the retest is a record of the block, not an event to peak for.
  • Write down week-11 loads. Block 2 week 1 starts from them.

Week 12

Feb 8 – 12
retesttape · month 3
  • Section 05 is the whole week. Reduced volume, one honest top set per main lift, best clean hold or rep count per ladder, splits support height, the full tape, and the five-minute longevity-marker battery from week 1.
  • Then stop and read the numbers before writing Block 2 (Section 11).

04Skill pacing over 12 weeks

The base document's timelines are the honest ones: planche 1–3 years, three strict wall HSPU 4–8 months, front split 3–12 months, side split 6–18 months. Twelve weeks is roughly a quarter of the fastest HSPU timeline and a tenth of the slowest planche one. So the question for this block is not "is the goal reached" but "is the ladder being climbed at a rate consistent with those ranges." 05Week 12: the retest protocol

The retest week compares the end of the block to the start of it, using numbers you already keep. It is run at about 60 % of normal set volume so that the measurements are taken fresh and the week doubles as the light week before Block 2. Nothing is maxed; "top set" below means the current working weight taken to 1 rep in reserve, exactly the same stopping point as every other set in the programme.

The five-minute longevity battery

Four tests that the mortality literature in Section 08 actually used or closely resembles, each cheap enough to do on the bedroom mat. They are recorded in week 1 and week 12 and then at every block retest. The point is explained in Section 08: at 23 you should ceiling most of them, and the job is to still ceiling them at 53.

  1. Sitting-rising test (SRT). Stand barefoot on the mat. Lower yourself to sitting cross-legged on the floor and stand back up, using as little support as you can. Score 5 for sitting and 5 for rising; subtract 1 for each hand, forearm, knee or side-of-leg touch used for support, and 0.5 for a visible wobble.record: score out of 10 · the published cohorts treat 8 or above as the top band
  2. 10-second one-leg stance. Eyes open, arms by your sides, free foot resting against the back of the standing leg's calf, looking straight ahead. Up to three attempts per side. Pass is 10 s without the free foot touching down.record: pass/fail each side · if both pass, also record best time to 30 s
  3. Standing toe reach. Feet together, knees straight, reach down slowly. Measure fingertip distance to the floor (positive) or past it (negative, hands flat on a block to measure).record: cm, signed
  4. Knee-to-wall ankle dorsiflexion. Half-kneeling or standing lunge facing a wall, test foot flat, drive the knee to the wall without the heel lifting. Slide the foot back until the knee can only just touch. Measure big toe to wall.record: cm each side · asymmetry over 2 cm is worth a note

The toe reach and knee-to-wall are stand-ins for two of the twenty joint movements in the Flexitest battery used by the Brazilian cohort; the full Flexitest requires a trained assessor and is not reproducible at home, so these are not Flexindex scores and should not be compared to the published ones.

Reading the block

  • Lifts. A returning lifter's first twelve weeks usually re-acquire old strength quickly; a load increase on every compound and a reps-at-the-same-load increase on the rest is a normal result, not a great one. The useful comparison is against your own weeks 1–2, not against any standard.
  • Ladders. Section 04's "week 12 plausible" column is the benchmark. One tier gained on HSPU and a stable or started tuck on the planche is on schedule.
  • Tape. Direction only, per the base document: waist down, shoulder and chest up, thigh holding, arm/calf/neck closer together. No ratio is a pass/fail.
  • Battery. Should be unchanged at the ceiling. If anything went backwards (a side that no longer balances, a toe reach that got worse while the splits improved), that is a joint-health signal worth more attention than any lift number.

06Warm-up, now with a pulse-raiser

The base document's daily layer is 5–10 minutes of CARs then light band external rotation. That is the specific-joint half of a warm-up and it stays exactly as written. What the base does not have is a general, whole-body half: a few minutes of easy movement that raises heart rate and tissue temperature before the joints are taken through range. Jeff Nippard's programme documents prescribe this as 5–10 minutes of low-intensity work raising heart rate to roughly 100–120 bpm (some of his programmes give 100–135), to the point of a light sweat, followed by 2–3 minutes of foam rolling on tight areas and dynamic drills, then ramp-up sets on the first lift. The heart-rate framing is a reasonable, conservative version of what the warm-up literature generally supports: raise temperature, rehearse the pattern, do not fatigue.

07CARs, PAILs and RAILs: the FRC source

The base document uses "CARs (controlled articular rotations)" as the daily joint layer without saying where the method comes from. It comes from one place: Functional Range Conditioning (FRC), the mobility system developed by Dr. Andreo Spina and taught through Functional Anatomy Seminars. CARs are FRC's foundational daily drill; PAILs and RAILs are FRC's formalised method of loaded end-range stretching. Making the connection explicit does two things for this programme: it turns the CARs line into a precise procedure instead of a vague "move each joint," and it gives the base document's loosely worded "light loaded stretching" in the Wednesday splits session a real, step-by-step protocol with sensible effort limits.

Evidence status, stated plainly

FRC is a coaching system, not a research programme. CARs are a structured form of active full-range joint movement; PAILs/RAILs are a structured form of the contract-relax (PNF) stretching the base document already uses, with a long passive hold and a ramped isometric. There are no large trials on PAILs/RAILs by name. What is well supported is the family they belong to: PNF and static stretching both reliably improve range of motion over weeks (Thomas et al., 2018; Ingram et al., 2024, Section 08), and long-duration end-range loading produces small strength and hypertrophy effects in the stretched muscle (Warneke et al., 2024). FRC is adopted here for its procedure and its safety structure, not because it has been shown to beat simpler stretching.

CARs, done the FRC way

FRC prescribes CARs as a daily "morning routine," one to two times a day, the joint-health equivalent of brushing teeth. The base document's 5–10 minute daily layer, every session and every rest day, is already that routine. The procedure:

  1. Isolate one joint. Everything else stays still; the rotation comes from the joint being trained, not from the spine or the neighbouring joint cheating range in.one joint at a time, both sides where paired
  2. Create light whole-body tension (irradiation). Brace the trunk, make loose fists, keep breathing. Around 20–30 % effort in the daily layer: enough to feel the joint doing the work, not a strain.irradiation 20–30 % · never hold the breath
  3. Move slowly to the painless outer limit. One full rotation takes 15–30 seconds. The goal is the largest circle today's joint can draw under control; when a spot catches or pinches, back off a little and keep the circle smooth.3–6 slow rotations per joint per direction
  4. Order and time. Neck, shoulders, elbows, wrists, spine (segmental if you can), hips, knees, ankles. At 3 reps per direction this is about 6 minutes; at 5 reps closer to 10. On Wednesday, wrist CARs get extra reps, as the base already specifies.5–10 min, fits the base layer exactly

PAILs and RAILs for the splits

PAILs (progressive angular isometric loading) is a ramped contraction of the muscle being stretched, pushing against the floor or the hand in the stretch position. RAILs (regressive angular isometric loading) immediately follows: a contraction of the opposite side of the joint, actively pulling deeper into the range. The sequence is PNF contract-relax with a long warm-in, a slow ramp, and an added active pull. In this programme it replaces, on a schedule, the "light loaded stretching (a plate on the thigh in the side split, hands on blocks in the front split)" line in the Wednesday splits session, and later the loaded element of Friday's.

  1. Passive hold. Settle into the front split (then side split) at the deepest position you can hold with the hips square and no joint pain. Breathe in for four, out for four. FRC practitioners use a minimum of two minutes here; this is the warm-in and should feel like letting go, not pushing.2 min minimum per position
  2. PAILs ramp. Irradiate the whole body, then drive the stretched leg into the floor (front split: front heel and back knee press down; side split: both legs press down and inward) ramping from about 10 % to the day's ceiling over 10–20 seconds. Hold the ceiling 5–15 seconds. The range does not change during PAILs; the tension does.ramp 10–20 s · hold 5–15 s · ceiling per schedule below
  3. RAILs, no release between. Switch, smoothly, to contracting the opposing muscles to pull yourself deeper (front split: pull the front heel back toward you and the back knee forward through the floor; side split: pull the legs apart). 5–15 seconds. Then exhale and settle into whatever new depth appeared.5–15 s · smooth transition, not a reset
  4. Rounds. One to three rounds per position, about 3–5 minutes each including the passive hold. The Wednesday 15–25 minute splits block holds two to three positions at two rounds comfortably.1–3 rounds per position

Safety, in order of strictness

FRC practitioners treat soreness lasting past 24–36 hours as the signal to reduce PAILs/RAILs intensity or frequency. The base document's rule is stricter about where and governs here: stretch sensation belongs in the muscle belly, and any pain at the knee, the inside of the hip joint or the sitting-bone tendon attachment is a 48-hour event that drops that position out for the week. The two rules stack; the stricter one wins at every point.

Kelly Starrett, for completeness

The other long-established figure in this space is Kelly Starrett (The Ready State; Built to Move, 2023, with Juliet Starrett). His framing is complementary rather than competing: ten self-tests ("vital signs") including getting up and down from the floor without hands, hip extension, shoulder range and single-leg balance, each with a benchmark and a daily 10-minute practice attached. Two of those tests (the floor sit-and-rise, the balance stand) are exactly the Araújo-group mortality predictors in Section 08, which is why they appear in the week-12 battery. Nothing else from Starrett's system is imported; the base programme already covers the daily movement practice he argues for.

08Flexibility, movement and longevity: the evidence

You asked for the actual studies. Here they are, with the numbers as published, followed by what they do and do not imply for a 23-year-old.

Flexibility and death: the Flexitest cohort

The study you will have seen referenced is Araújo et al., 2024, Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports: a prospective cohort of 3,139 adults aged 46–65 (2,087 men, 1,052 women) assessed at the CLINIMEX exercise-medicine clinic in Rio de Janeiro with the Flexitest, a 20-movement passive range-of-motion battery scored 0–4 per movement and summed into a "Flexindex" (0–80). Over a mean 12.9 years of follow-up there were 302 deaths (9.6 %). Survivors' Flexindex was about 10 % higher than non-survivors' in both sexes. Comparing the least flexible to the most flexible, after adjusting for age, body-mass index and health status, mortality risk was 1.87 times higher in men and 4.78 times higher in women. Women scored about 35 % higher on the Flexindex than men overall.

Not a flexibility finding, included because the "movement quality for longevity" case rests on strength, flexibility and balance together, and the lifting half of this programme is the strength input.
What these studies do not show

Every result above is observational. None of them is a trial in which people were made more flexible or stronger and then lived longer. Low flexibility, poor floor-rising and poor balance in a 60-year-old are plausibly markers of the same things that kill people (sarcopenia, frailty, inactivity, neurological decline), not independent causes. The authors of the 2024 paper say as much and call for flexibility to be assessed routinely; they do not claim stretching extends life. Also, every cohort is middle-aged or older. There is no cohort of 23-year-olds, because almost no 23-year-old fails these tests, and almost none die during follow-up.

What it means at 23

Three things, none of which require changing the programme and all of which argue for keeping its priorities.

  • The markers are a floor to protect, not a target to chase. You should score 10 on the sitting-rising test and pass the balance test on both legs now. The useful fact is that half of 70-year-olds cannot; the decline between here and there is the thing to prevent, and the earliest possible baseline (week 1) is how you will know if it starts.
  • The programme already trains the mechanism behind every marker. Floor-rising is hip and ankle range plus relative leg strength: the splits, the squat pattern and CARs. One-leg balance is ankle control and hip stability: single-leg RDLs, Bulgarian split squats, ankle CARs. Grip and knee-extension strength: deadlifts, pull-ups, squats. Nothing new is needed; the longevity case is a second reason for what the base document already prescribes.
  • Joint health as Priority 1 is the longevity position. A programme that trades a wrist or a hip for a faster planche is also trading the 50-year version of these tests. The base document's override is, read this way, a longevity rule.

How much stretching, revisited for the longevity angle

The dosing literature the base splits section drew on is consistent and worth stating precisely, because it bears on the "gentle daily touch" line more than on the intense sessions.

  • Thomas et al., 2018 (Int J Sports Med, systematic review of chronic stretching): total weekly time per muscle was what predicted range-of-motion gains, with a threshold around 5 minutes per week; time within a single session did not matter once the weekly total was met; weekly frequency was positively associated with gains, with the authors suggesting at least 5 days a week; and static stretching produced larger long-term gains than ballistic or PNF.
  • Ingram et al., 2024 (Sports Medicine, 189 studies, 6,654 adults, mean age about 27): static stretching has a moderate acute effect (g = 0.63) and a large chronic effect (g = 0.96) on flexibility. Gains plateaued at about 4 minutes per muscle group per session and about 10 minutes per muscle group per week, with no extra benefit beyond that. Stretch intensity, session frequency, intervention length, age, sex and training status did not moderate the effect; people with poor baseline flexibility gained more.
  • Behm et al., 2023 (Sports Medicine – Open, 47 studies): a single bout of stretching of any type produces a small acute range-of-motion increase; the type matters less than doing it.
  • Warneke et al., 2024 (Sports Medicine – Open): long-duration static stretching produces small but real increases in maximal strength (d ≈ 0.30) and muscle size (d ≈ 0.20) in the stretched muscle, and only when the dose is long and frequent. Relevant to the splits, not a reason to stretch instead of lift.

What this means for the programme. The 3–5 minute gentle daily touch per split position already clears both the Thomas threshold (5 min/week) and the Ingram plateau (10 min/week) on its own, seven days a week, which is the high-frequency pattern Thomas found most effective. That is the longevity-relevant dose: a modest amount, every day, forever. The three intense sessions on top of it are for a different goal, a full split, which is an extreme end-range outcome the general stretching literature was not designed to test; Ingram's "no benefit beyond 10 minutes" is a finding about average flexibility gains in average people, not a ceiling on what full splits require. The base document's framing stands exactly: the intense-session cap is a conservative coaching heuristic, the daily touch is the evidence-backed floor, and if you ever had to keep only one of the two, keep the daily touch.

09Nippard, frequency and the age mismatch

Jeff Nippard is a natural bodybuilder whose programmes and videos cite the exercise-science literature, and two of his positions were raised for this block. One transfers cleanly (the warm-up, Section 06). The other needs to be handled carefully because it was built for a different person.

What could and could not be verified

  • Warm-up: verified from his own programme documents. 5–10 minutes raising heart rate to about 100–120 bpm (100–135 in some programmes), a light sweat, optional 2–3 minutes of foam rolling on tight areas, dynamic drills, ramp-up sets. Adopted in Section 06.
  • Lower-frequency "longevity" split for natural lifters over 30, with a "96-hour recovery curve": only partly verifiable. His Essentials programme (jeffnippard.com) does offer a 3-day option and argues for lower volume at higher intensity for time-constrained lifters, and his public commentary on frequency is consistent with "fewer days is fine if volume is kept." But the specific "30+, 3 days a week, 96-hour recovery curve" phrasing turned up only on third-party summary sites, not in a primary Nippard video or document that could be checked. It is treated below as a plausible paraphrase of his position, not a quotation.
  • "Science Based Movers": no channel of that exact name could be found. The real, checkable lineage of the "science-based movement" coaching world used here is Spina's FRC (Section 07) and Starrett's The Ready State. If you had a specific creator in mind, send the link and this section gets corrected.

The age mismatch, addressed directly

You are 23, turning 24. A guideline written for natural lifters over 30, aimed at protecting joints across decades by training each pattern less often, does not transfer to you as a frequency rule, and importing it would quietly contradict the base document's five-day structure, which was chosen for other, good reasons. Three points make the decision clear:

  • The evidence on frequency does not favour three days for your situation. Once weekly volume is matched, the hypertrophy difference between training a muscle twice versus three or more times a week is small (Pelland et al., 2024 meta-regression; the earlier Schoenfeld frequency reviews point the same way). Five sessions of 45–60 minutes with every pattern twice a week is simply the way the base document fits 10–20 sets per muscle into the available time. Three longer sessions would hit the same volume with more fatigue per session, which is worse for skill practice and no better for joints.
  • The underlying principle is already in the base document, applied per joint rather than per week. The reason to lower frequency in an older lifter is connective-tissue recovery. The base handles that directly: pressing shoulder and extended wrist are loaded on three non-adjacent days with at least 48 hours between heavy exposures, pull-and-lower days sit in between, hard splits are capped at three non-consecutive sessions, and the 48-hour rule catches anything that slips through. That is a joint-recovery design; it does not need to be a lower-frequency one.
  • The transferable part is the attitude, and it is adopted. Nippard's longevity argument is that natural lifters should plan for decades, not months, and keep set quality high rather than piling on volume. The base document's "repeat, don't rotate," its volume at the lower-middle of the evidence range for the lower body, its 1–3 reps shy of failure, and this block's early deload are the same attitude in a 23-year-old's calendar. If, at 30-plus, a joint starts needing more than 48 hours, the right move is the one the base already describes (drop a Friday Push B, not a pull day) and at that point a four-day version of the same week becomes the standing structure. Not now.

11After week 12

The honest answer, and the one consistent with the base document's "repeat, don't rotate" principle: re-measure everything, start Block 2 with the updated baselines, same five-day week.

  • Block 2 week 1 begins the Monday after the retest, at the week-11 loads and reps (not the retest top sets, which were single sets taken fresh). It is a loading week, not a placement week; placement was a Block 1 feature.
  • Same structure, same ladders, same rules. Mon Push A, Tue Lower + Pull A, Wed Skill, Thu Lower + Pull B, Fri Push B. Deload at week 7, retest at week 12. The only structural edits allowed between blocks are the ones the base document already names as responses to specific findings: a fifth squat set if the squat stalled, two sets of curls on Thursday if the arm has not moved on three consecutive tapes, moving a lift one column left on the substitution ladder when equipment arrives.
  • Updated baselines. The week-12 tape becomes the "now" column for the base document's ratio reads. The arm = calf = neck convergence point is re-set at the new neck measurement, as the base specifies. The week-12 battery scores become the floor to protect for the next block.
  • PAILs/RAILs may go to full effort on Wednesday and Friday in Block 2 only if Block 1 produced zero hip or knee 48-hour events in weeks 4–11. Otherwise they stay at 70 % for another block.
  • What would justify a bigger change. Only a Priority 1 event: a recurring ache in the same joint across two blocks despite the 48-hour rule. That is a signal to remove a movement, not to redesign the week. Variety-seeking is not a reason, by your own instruction.

Block 2 is therefore not a new document. It is this one with the dates moved forward twelve weeks and the week-1 numbers replaced by the week-12 ones.
