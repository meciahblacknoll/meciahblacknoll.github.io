Mobility Skill Lines

Two new skills you can actually win in months rather than years, the pistol squat and the back bridge, in the mould of Elliot Kim's Strong & Mobile work; numeric strength benchmarks for push-ups, pull-ups and dips with a one-time test so your real numbers get logged instead of guessed; where all of it fits in the five-day week without blowing the time budget; and why the two skills are the best real-world demonstration of the longevity capacities Block 1 already documents.

01What this adds, and what it leaves alone

The base document (Bedroom Training Program v2) settles what you do; Block 1 settles when. Neither is re-opened here. The three goals and their priority order, the five-day week, double progression, the tier rule, the deload at week 7, the retest at week 12, the 48-hour override, the FRC procedure and the splits cap all stand exactly as written. Where this page and either of them disagree, they are right and this page has a bug.

What this page adds is an answer to a gap that was real. Every skill goal currently in the program takes a long time: three strict handstand push-ups 4–8 months, a front split 3–12 months, a side split 6–18 months, a full planche 1–3 years. Those are honest numbers and they stay. But there is nothing on the skill line that gives a real, trackable win inside a block or two, and a program you repeat for years needs some of those, especially in the first six months when a returning lifter's barbell numbers are the only thing visibly moving.

The two skills chosen fill that gap and serve Priority 1 at the same time. The pistol squat (a full-depth single-leg squat) is ankle, knee and hip range under load plus single-leg balance. The back bridge (hands and feet on the floor, hips high, spine and shoulders in loaded extension) is the one position almost nothing else in the program trains: spinal extension and overhead shoulder range, loaded. Both have a realistic path measured in months, both have named tiers with pass criteria in the same format as the base document's ladders, and both are the kind of movement the longevity research in Block 1 Section 08 is about.

How these relate to the three goals

The base document's Priority 2 card names four skills (front split, side split, full planche, 3+ strict wall HSPU). This page does not rewrite that card. The pistol squat and back bridge are added as tracked skill lines under Priority 2, below the four named goals in importance: if a week is short, the four original ladders keep their sets and these two lose theirs first. They earn their place by also being Priority 1 work (joint range under control), which is why they are worth the two minutes a week they cost.

Still the override

Any new ache that lingers past 48 hours drops that movement back one tier, or out of the session entirely, for that week. For the pistol that means knee or ankle; for the bridge that means the low back, the neck, the front of the shoulder or the wrist. Nothing on this page outranks it.

03Pistol squat ladder

Realistic timeline to one clean full-depth pistol each side: 3–6 months for a returning lifter who passes the ankle gate at the start; 6–12 months if the ankle gate fails first, because the ankle has to be earned before the strength matters. These are consensus coaching ranges from several independent progression guides, not trial data. Trained Tuesday (Section 06). Tiers 1–2 are climbed with the base document's tier rule (3–4 sets of 8–12 clean reps). Tiers 3–5 state lower rep criteria on purpose: a full-depth single-leg rep is strength-limited the way a weighted lift is, and 3 × 5 clean is the honest equivalent of the rule there.

  1. Ankle gate (not a tier, a prerequisite). The knee-to-wall dorsiflexion test you already log in Block 1 week 1 and week 12: foot flat, drive the knee to the wall without the heel lifting, measure big toe to wall. Full-depth pistols need roughly 35–45° of dorsiflexion, which coaching guides translate to about 10–12 cm on this test. Also: a full-depth two-leg bodyweight squat with heels down. ≥ 10 cm each side and a heels-down full squat. Below 10 cm, the pistol stays at tier 1 regardless of strength, and ankle CARs get extra reps in the daily layer until it passes.Source: general (knee-to-wall figure is a coaching heuristic repeated across pistol guides, not a validated cutoff; the test itself is the one Block 1 adopted).
  2. Bench pistol. Stand on one leg in front of the flat bench (about 45 cm), other leg held straight out front, arms reached forward. Sit back to the bench under control, no drop, then stand without the free foot touching down. 3 × 8 per leg, clean: heel down, knee tracking over the foot, no plop onto the bench.Source: general (box pistol is the universal first step).
  3. Low-box pistol. Same movement to a lower target, about 25–30 cm (stacked plates, a stack of books, a step). Depth is the variable; everything else is the same. 3 × 8 per leg at ~25 cm, clean.Source: general.
  4. Assisted full-depth pistol. Full depth now: hamstring touches the calf at the bottom, the usual definition of a full pistol and the depth Kim tests. Assistance is either one hand on a ring or the doorframe, used as lightly as possible, or a light counterweight held out front (a 5–10 lb plate or the adjustable dumbbell set light), which shifts your centre of mass forward and is what makes the bottom position balanceable. Kim's cues apply: arms reached forward, shoulders protracted, free leg's toes pointed. 3 × 5 per leg, full depth, heel down, counterweight ≤ 10 lb or fingertip assistance only.Source: cues from Kim (verified write-up); the depth definition and the counterweight method are general.
  5. Full pistol. No assistance, no counterweight, arms forward, full depth, stand up without the heel lifting or the free foot touching. 1 clean rep each side: goal reached. Then build to 3 × 5 per leg.Source: general.
  6. Weighted pistol. Dumbbell held at the chest, goblet-style. Open-ended; double progression as for any loaded lift. ongoing. This is where the 35 lb adjustable dumbbells, which the base document says are too light for main lifts, get a genuine long-term job without buying anything.Source: general.
Joint rule, pistol edition

Pain at the front of the knee, the inside of the knee, or the front of the ankle at the bottom position is a 48-hour event, not a mobility sensation to push through. Hamstring, glute and quad fatigue is fine. If the heel lifts to reach depth, that is the ankle gate failing in real time: go back to the box tier that depth allows, do not force the heel.

Why this skill is plausibly fast for you. You already train Bulgarian split squats on Thursday, you have a squat pattern twice a week, and you are a returning lifter with prior barbell experience. The strength to pistol is usually there before the ankle range and the balance are; the ladder is mostly about earning the bottom position. Placement in Block 1 week 2 (Section 07) will put you at tier 1 or 2 and tell you which of the two it is.

04Back bridge ladder

Realistic timeline to a full bridge (hands and feet on the floor, hips high, head off the floor, elbows allowed to bend): 2–5 months. To a wheel-quality bridge (arms straight, shoulders stacked over the wrists, feet walked in): 5–12 months. These ranges are thinner than the pistol's: the bridge has no cohort or trial data on time-to-skill at all, and the figures are what the coaching progressions imply when each step takes its usual weeks. Treat them as a sketch. Trained Wednesday (Section 06), inside the mobility block, after the strength skills.

The step order is the Convict Conditioning bridge series (Paul Wade) as reproduced in Hybrid Calisthenics' free progression, which is the most widely used version of it. Their rep standards are given alongside each tier for reference; the pass criteria actually used here are the base document's tier rule (3–4 sets of 8–12 clean, or a clean hold), deliberately lower-rep than the source because high-rep bridging is endurance work and the point here is end-range control.

  1. Glute bridge (short bridge). On your back, knees bent, feet flat, press the hips up until the trunk is a straight line from knees to shoulders, squeeze at the top. 3 × 12 with a 2-second top squeeze. (Hybrid Calisthenics standard: 3 × 50.)Source: Convict Conditioning / Hybrid Calisthenics.
  2. Straight bridge (reverse plank). Sit with legs straight, hands on the floor behind you, press the hips up until the body is one straight line from heels to shoulders, head neutral. First real shoulder-extension load. 3 × 12 with a 2-second hold. (HC: 3 × 30.)Source: CC / HC.
  3. Wall bridge. Stand with your back to a wall about a step away. Reach overhead and back, place the hands on the wall, and walk them down a few hand-widths and back up. Eyes on the hands throughout. This teaches the extension pattern standing, where you can bail at any time. 3 × 8 walking the hands to roughly hip height and back up, under control. (HC: 3 × 30.)Source: CC / HC. Eye-line cue from Kim.
  4. Incline bridge. Hands on the flat bench, feet on the floor, press up into a bridge with the hips high. The bench takes some range out of the shoulders so the spine and hips learn the shape first. Hands flat, fingers pointing toward the feet, eyes on the hands. 3 × 8 with a 3-second hold at the top, arms as straight as they will go. (HC: 2 × 25.)Source: CC / HC. Kim's own regression is the mirror of this (heels on a bench, neutral grip); the hands-on-bench version is used because it builds the shoulder range the full bridge needs.
  5. Full bridge. Hands and feet on the floor, hips pressed high, head off the floor, eyes on the hands. Elbows may bend. 3 × 5 with a 5-second hold: "full bridge" reached. (HC: 2 × 15.)Source: CC / HC.
  6. Wheel-quality bridge. Same, but arms straight and shoulders stacked over or slightly past the wrists, feet walked in toward the hands. This is the gymnastics bridge / yoga wheel (urdhva dhanurasana). 3 holds of 15–20 seconds, arms locked: goal reached. Beyond this (one-leg bridges, wall walks down and up, stand-to-stand) is open-ended and not programmed here.Source: CC / HC for the step; the straight-arm, shoulders-over-wrists standard is the gymnastics/yoga definition of the position.
One step deliberately skipped

The Convict Conditioning series has a head bridge (crown of the head on the floor, like a wrestler's bridge) between the incline and full bridge. It is left out here on Priority 1 grounds: it loads the cervical spine in extension under bodyweight, which is not a joint this program has any reason to load that way, and Hybrid Calisthenics' own note on it is "avoid this exercise if it consistently causes back pain." The incline bridge on a progressively lower surface does the same job: if the jump from the bench to the floor is too big, put the hands on a stack of plates or thick books and lower it a few centimetres at a time.

Joint rule, bridge edition

The stretch belongs in the front of the hips, the abdominals, the chest and the front of the shoulders. A pinch at the base of the spine, anything in the neck, or pain at the front of the shoulder joint or in the wrist is a 48-hour event. Breathe out into the position, the same in-for-four, out-for-four breathing Block 1's PAILs procedure uses. Never hold the breath in a bridge.

05Strength benchmarks and the one-time test

The base document prescribes pull-ups, dips and pressing by rep range and double progression, but never says where your numbers sit on any named scale. That makes "ambitious yet realistic" hard to judge for anything but the skills. This section adds a scale and a test. It does not add any set to the program: the test replaces an existing set in one week, and the benchmark is read from the log afterwards.

The scale

Two general calisthenics standards references were checked. They agree on the shape (beginner / intermediate / advanced by strict reps in one set) and disagree on exact cut-points, which is normal: these are coaching conventions, not measured population percentiles, and no source gives a derivation. The Playthenics ladder is adopted because it has the tightest criteria stated ("reps in one strict set, bodyweight only; a kipped chin-over-bar or a half push-up does not count") and because the base document already uses Steven Low's tier logic, which these numbers sit comfortably beside. The second source (Cinnabar Calisthenics) is shown so the variance is visible.

Playthenics' full ladder has sub-steps (Beginner II at 24 push-ups / 7 pull-ups / 7 dips, Intermediate II at 34 / 14 / 14, and so on up to Advanced III at 50 / 28 / 28); only the three entry points are shown because that is enough to place yourself. A claim that was checked and dropped: an earlier note for this page gave "+25 % / +50 % bodyweight loaded" as pull-up equivalents for the intermediate and advanced tiers. Neither source states any such equivalence, so it is not used. What Playthenics does say is that past 20 strict reps you add load on a belt and work in the 5–8 rep range, which is consistent with the base document's own rule of adding a backpack once 12 clean pull-ups are easy.

Where you probably are, and why it is not written down

The base document's starting point is "returning lifter: push-ups and pull-ups are fine, prior barbell experience, just out of practice." On this scale that is most likely the beginner-to-intermediate band: somewhere between 5 and 12 strict pull-ups, 20 and 30 strict push-ups, and on dips probably the low end because ring dips are harder than the bar dips the standard assumes. That is a guess. It is not entered anywhere as your number. The test below produces the real one.

The one-time test: Block 1 week 2, and week 2 of every block after

Week 1 of Block 1 is already full of placement tests; week 2 is the "add reps, film your lifts" week and has room. Each test is the first set of a lift already in that day's session, taken as a strict max-rep set instead of the usual 6–12 working set, and the remaining sets run as normal. No session gets longer.

06Where they live in the week

Both skills need a weekly slot, every session already runs at about 55–60 minutes, and the base document's joint rules constrain the choice more than the clock does. The decisions, then the two session cards with only the changed lines marked.

Decisions

  • Pistol squat on Tuesday, not Thursday. It is a knee-dominant squat pattern, so it belongs on a lower day, and Tuesday's quad lift is the bilateral squat while Thursday already has the unilateral Bulgarian split squat. Putting the pistol on Tuesday gives each lower day one bilateral and one single-leg knee movement instead of stacking both single-leg movements on Thursday, which is also the longer list of the two. The joint-load map does not change: Tuesday knee and hip were already "heavy."
  • Pistol first, as the squat's warm-up. The base document says skill is practised fresh, and the early tiers are low-fatigue bodyweight work. So the pistol sets go straight after the daily layer and replace the squat's general ramp-up (the base's "goblet squat as warm-up only" line and Block 1's 2–3 minutes of ramp-up sets). One light set on the bar itself stays. Three sets per leg, alternating legs with 90 seconds' rest after each pair, is about 7–8 minutes; minus the 2–3 minutes of ramp-up it replaces, the pistol adds roughly 4–5 minutes on its own.
  • Wrist prehab moves into the pistol rest periods. Tuesday's four light wrist sets currently sit at the end of the session on their own clock (about 4 minutes). Supersetting them with the pistol sets costs nothing: the wrists are fresh, the work is light, and the three 90-second pistol rests hold four 30–40-second prehab sets comfortably. This is the explicit trim that pays for the pistol: it recovers about 4 of the 4–5 minutes the pistol adds. Tuesday's total stays at about 55–60 minutes, roughly a minute longer than before on an average day.
  • Back bridge on Wednesday, inside the mobility block, after the strength skills. Two reasons it must be Wednesday or another press day and not Tuesday or Thursday: the full bridge loads the wrist in extension (hands flat, fingers toward the feet) and the shoulder overhead, and the base document's whole week is built so that those loads land on Mon / Wed / Fri only, never on the pull-and-lower days between. Wednesday is the lowest-fatigue of the three, is the day that already holds the PAILs/RAILs end-range work the bridge resembles, and the bridge is the natural counter-shape to a session spent in the planche's protracted, hollow position. It goes last: the planche and HSPU ladders are higher-priority skills and get the fresh nervous system.
  • The explicit trim on Wednesday: the intense splits block gives up 5–6 minutes. Wednesday's splits are "15–25 min" in the base. The bridge's 2–3 sets take about 5–6 minutes of that, so Wednesday's splits run 12–20 minutes from now on, with the bridge inside the same envelope. The cost falls on the full-split timeline, slightly, and only on Wednesday: Monday and Friday splits are untouched, the gentle daily touch (which is the evidence-dosed part per Block 1 Section 08) is untouched, and the Thomas and Ingram weekly thresholds are still cleared by the daily touch alone. Wednesday's total stays at 55–60.
  • No second weekly bridge day, on purpose. Most calisthenics templates train the bridge once or twice a week. Adding a second exposure would mean either Monday or Friday growing (both are at budget) or putting loaded spinal and wrist extension on a deadlift day. One quality session a week is the Priority 1 choice. If the bridge ladder stalls for a full block with a clean log, the first fix is a single set of the current tier added to Friday after the HSPU ladder, not a new day.
  • Joint-load map deltas. Tuesday: none. Wednesday: spine goes from "—" to light extension, and the already-"heavy" wrist column gets a few more extension reps from tier 4 upward. Because of that, on Wednesday the drop order for a sore wrist is: bridge first, then (per the base) wrist prehab on Tue/Thu, before any main lift or the planche ladder is touched.
  • Pattern frequency and volume. The squat pattern goes from 2 to 3 exposures a week (Tue squat, Tue pistol, Thu split squat), still inside "at least twice." Direct quad sets go from 8 to 11, inside the 10–20 range. This is not the base document's "fifth squat set on a stall" rule being triggered early; it is bodyweight skill work that happens to count. If the Tuesday squat's double progression slows after the pistol is added, drop the pistol to 2 sets before touching anything else.

When a week only has four slots

The base rule stands: drop Friday Push B, never Wednesday, never a pull day. Within a kept session, if time runs short, these two skills lose their sets before any of the four original ladders or any loaded lift does (Section 01). On a deload week both are held at the current tier at half the sets, like every other ladder.

08The longevity connection

Block 1 Section 08 lays out the evidence that, in middle-aged and older cohorts, poor flexibility, poor floor-rising, poor one-leg balance and low muscular strength each predict earlier death, with all the observational caveats stated there, which carry over unchanged here. It also makes the point that at 23 those markers are a floor to protect, not a target to chase, and that the program already trains the mechanism behind each one. The two new skills are the most direct demonstration of those mechanisms in the whole program, and that is the real reason they earn a slot, over and above being trackable wins.

These are the ranges the rest of the program leaves nearly untouched (the deadlift and RDL load the spine heavily but in neutral, not through extension range, and the overhead press and HSPU load the shoulder overhead as strength work, not as a loaded end-range stretch), so the bridge is the one movement that extends the program's coverage of the Flexitest's range rather than deepening something already covered.
Starrett's "vital signs" (Block 1 Section 07): floor sit-and-rise, hip extension, shoulder range, single-leg balanceSelf-tests with benchmarks and a daily practice attachedBoth. The pistol is the sit-and-rise and the balance test; the bridge is hip extension and shoulder range in one position. Nothing further from Starrett is imported, exactly as Block 1 decided.
The same caveats, restated

None of this says a pistol squat or a bridge makes you live longer. The cohort studies are observational, the subjects are 46 and older, and the markers are plausibly signals of the underlying things that kill people rather than causes. What the two skills do is train and measure the exact capacities those studies found to matter, at a level far above the floor the studies tested, and keep a record. Block 1's framing stands: the job is to ceiling these tests at 23 and still ceiling them at 53, and a logged pistol tier and bridge tier are a much more sensitive early-warning instrument for that than a pass/fail on a 10-second stance.

And the FRC connection. Block 1 Section 07 adopted CARs, PAILs and RAILs as the procedure for building range and then owning it with strength. Kim's method, in his own words, is the same idea applied to skills: increase the range, then build strength throughout it. The bridge in particular is a loaded end-range hold for the hip flexors, abdominals, chest and anterior shoulder, which is structurally the same kind of work as a PAILs hold in the split, aimed at a different set of joints. If you ever want a PAILs/RAILs version of the bridge (press the hands and feet into the floor to contract into the stretch, then actively pull the hips higher), it is the obvious next step, but it waits until tier 5 is reached and the Block 2 rule on full-effort PAILs/RAILs is met. Nothing here changes that rule.
